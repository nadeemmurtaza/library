# Vaporwave UI design style

Pastel neon, digital sunsets, classical sculpture, grids, obsolete consumer imagery, and surreal 1980s–1990s nostalgia.

## Design overview

## History and origins

A fact-checked account of where this visual language came from, with links to the references used.

Vaporwave formed around experimental electronic releases in the early 2010s and quickly developed a shared visual language of obsolete consumer media, corporate calm, classical sculpture, Japanese text, malls, and corrupted nostalgia. Artists including James Ferraro, Daniel Lopatin, and Ramona Andra Xavier—whose Macintosh Plus project became emblematic—were central; the style often works as critique and uneasy fascination, not simple 1980s appreciation.

## Design principles and implementation

Use these principles to write a focused AI UI design prompt instead of asking for a vague visual treatment.

Use pastel pink, lavender, cyan, peach, and deep violet with neon sunsets, faded gradients, and cool digital shadow. In a daisyUI theme, use pastel pink or cyan for `primary`, lavender or peach for `secondary`, and step `base-100`, `base-200`, and `base-300` through deep violet, digital navy, and cool near-black.

Choose classical busts, obsolete consumer goods, malls, palm trees, Japanese text accents, low-resolution skies, and surreal desktop artifacts.

Keep copy low to medium and visual space broad, allowing nostalgia, repetition, and atmosphere to dominate before detailed information.

Drift objects slowly, scroll perspective grids, degrade video with VHS texture, and loop sunsets or statues with detached dreamlike pacing.

Use wireframe grids, floating windows, bust silhouettes, columns, palms, checker planes, and simple three-dimensional primitives. In daisyUI themes, set the `--radius-*` variables to `0` or `0.25rem`.

Pair a classical serif or retro display face with a clean sans-serif, using Japanese characters only when linguistically correct and contextually meaningful.
